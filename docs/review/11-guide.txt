Guide to measurements

You do not need medical training to use Keel. This page explains each reading: what it is, how to take it properly, and why spaceflight changes it.

The five hazards of human spaceflight, and what Keel watches for each
Hazard	What it does	What Keel tracks
Space radiation	Cosmic rays and solar storms. Mostly a long-term risk; a solar storm can also matter within hours.	Daily and mission-total dose against the 600 mSv career limit; an alert with the shelter procedure when the dose rate jumps.
Isolation and confinement	Mood and sleep problems become more likely the longer a small crew is shut in together.	Private mood check-ins, sleep duration and timing, reaction self-test, each against your own normal.
Distance from Earth	Up to about 24 minutes of signal delay each way, blackouts, no resupply and no quick return.	Everything runs on board. The link only carries a summary you approve, when a link exists.
Gravity fields	Without weight, bone and muscle are lost, fluid shifts toward the head, and the body must re-adapt at each gravity change.	Exercise against plan, resting heart rate and HRV; normal ranges are widened and re-learned after each gravity change.
Hostile, closed environment	Recycled air and water, shared microbes, an immune system that behaves differently.	Cabin carbon dioxide with the levels linked to headaches; early signs of infection from heart rate, HRV and temperature.

When to involve someone else
Now, whatever Keel shows: chest pain or pressure, shortness of breath at rest, severe side or back pain, fainting, a resting oxygen reading of 92% or lower. Tell the medical officer.
Today: when Keel shows "Act today" with the medical officer level, or a temperature of 38.0 °C or more.
When a link opens: anything the medical officer wants a flight surgeon to review. Keel keeps the summary queued.

These are general thresholds from public clinical guidance, not a flight rule.

Brain, sleep and mood

Isolation and confinement, plus a lost day-night cycle, disturb sleep and wear on mood and attention.

Sleep duration (h)
See my trend

Hours actually asleep in the last sleep period.

How to measure. Measured by the wearable. If you did not wear it, enter your best estimate at check-in.

Why it matters. Crews sleep less in space than on Earth. Short sleep builds up as a debt that slows reactions and raises error risk.

Sleep timing shift (h)
See my trend

How far the middle of your sleep moved from your scheduled sleep period. Zero means you slept when planned.

How to measure. Calculated from when you fell asleep and woke up.

Why it matters. When sleep slides away from the schedule, your body clock and the mission clock disagree, which hurts sleep quality and alertness.

Reaction time (ms)
See my trend

Your average response time in the reaction self-test.

How to measure. Tap as soon as the target appears. Do it at about the same time each day, sitting still, before caffeine if you can.

Why it matters. Reaction time is a sensitive, objective sign of fatigue. It slows with sleep loss and may slow with high cabin carbon dioxide.

Mood (/5)
See my trend

Your own rating of how you feel today, from 1 (very low) to 5 (very good).

How to measure. Answer quickly and honestly at check-in. There is no right answer, and you decide who can see it.

Why it matters. Isolation, confinement and distance from home wear on everyone. A slow slide is easier to turn around when it is noticed early.

Heart and circulation

Without gravity, blood shifts toward the head and the heart is loaded differently. Radiation and inactivity add long-term risk.

Resting heart rate (bpm)
See my trend

How fast your heart beats when you are fully at rest, usually measured during sleep.

How to measure. Wear the wrist or chest sensor overnight. Keel takes the lowest stable stretch while you sleep, so exercise and coffee do not distort it.

Why it matters. A resting heart rate that climbs above your own normal is one of the earliest whole-body signs of infection, dehydration, poor recovery or loss of fitness.

Heart rate variability (ms)
See my trend

The small beat-to-beat changes in your heart rhythm (RMSSD). Higher usually means your body is recovering well.

How to measure. Measured automatically overnight by the wearable, or in a two-minute still reading with the chest strap.

Why it matters. HRV drops when the body is under strain: illness, short sleep, heavy training or stress. It often moves before you feel anything.

Blood pressure (upper) (mmHg)
See my trend

The upper (systolic) number of your blood pressure.

How to measure. Sit still for five minutes, cuff on the upper arm at heart level, do not talk during the reading.

Why it matters. Blood pressure regulation changes without gravity. Readings well outside your normal deserve a repeat and a word with the medical officer.

Lungs and oxygen

A sealed cabin recycles its own air: carbon dioxide can build up and dust does not settle.

Blood oxygen (%)
See my trend

The share of your red blood cells carrying oxygen.

How to measure. Overnight from the wearable, or a 30-second fingertip reading: warm hand, hold still, wait for a steady number.

Why it matters. A sustained drop can point to a breathing or lung problem or a cabin atmosphere issue.

Food and fluids

Crews eat and drink less than they need in space, which speeds up body mass loss and raises kidney-stone risk.

Fluid intake (L)
See my trend

How much you drank today.

How to measure. Tap once per drink bag at check-in, or enter litres.

Why it matters. Crews tend to drink too little in space. Low intake raises the risk of kidney stones and makes the heart work harder.

Food intake (% of plan)
See my trend

How much of your planned food you ate today.

How to measure. Estimate at check-in: all, most, about half, little.

Why it matters. Under-eating is common in space and speeds up loss of body mass, muscle and bone.

Blood sugar (mg/dL)
See my trend

The sugar level in a fingertip drop of blood.

How to measure. Clean fingertip, first drop wiped away, measure before breakfast for a comparable number.

Why it matters. Named in the challenge as a useful indicator. Keel shows it against your own previous readings and a general reference range.

Muscle and bone

Weight-bearing bones lose about 1 to 1.5% of their density per month without countermeasures. Daily loading is the defence.

Exercise, 7-day average (min/day)
See my trend

Average minutes of exercise per day over the last week.

How to measure. Logged by the exercise devices or entered at check-in.

Why it matters. Daily exercise is the main protection against losing bone, muscle and heart fitness without gravity. Missed sessions add up quietly.

Whole body

Immune changes in flight make infections and reactivated viruses more likely; temperature is an early sign.

Body temperature (°C)
See my trend

Your body temperature, from the wearable skin sensor overnight or a thermometer reading.

How to measure. For a spot check use the thermometer at the same time each day, before exercise and before eating or drinking.

Why it matters. A rise above your own normal supports the picture of infection or inflammation; body temperature also runs differently in space.

Body mass (kg)
See my trend

Your body mass, measured on the mass-measurement device (a normal scale does not work without gravity).

How to measure. Weekly, same time of day, before eating.

Why it matters. Steady loss of body mass signals under-eating or dehydration.

Cabin and radiation

Beyond Earth's magnetic shield, cosmic rays give a steady dose and solar storms an occasional burst.

Cabin carbon dioxide (mmHg)
See my trend

The average carbon dioxide level in the cabin air over the last day.

How to measure. Read automatically from the cabin air monitor.

Why it matters. In a closed cabin, exhaled carbon dioxide builds up. Higher levels are linked to headaches and may dull thinking.

Radiation dose (mSv/day)
See my trend

The radiation dose your personal dosimeter recorded today, and your running mission total.

How to measure. Read automatically from your personal dosimeter. Keep it on you.

Why it matters. Beyond Earth's magnetic shield the crew receives a steady dose from cosmic rays and occasional bursts from solar storms. The total is tracked against a career limit.
