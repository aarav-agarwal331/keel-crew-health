The idea

On the International Space Station a flight surgeon on Earth watches crew health in near real time. On the way to Mars the signal takes minutes each way and sometimes there is no link at all, so the crew has to notice and act on health changes themselves.

Keel learns what is normal for each person, notices when their readings drift away from that, says what changed in plain words, and points to one documented action. All of it runs on the device. The crew member owns the data and chooses what the medical officer, the commander and the ground can see.

Keel is decision support, not a medical device. It never states a diagnosis. It says "this is unusual for you" and "this is what the documented countermeasure is".

What is measured, and why
Indicator	Unit	Comes from	In the automatic alert?	Risk it tracks
Resting heart rate	bpm	Wearable	Yes	A resting heart rate that climbs above your own normal is one of the earliest whole-body signs of infection, dehydration, poor recovery or loss of fitness.
Heart rate variability	ms	Wearable	Yes	HRV drops when the body is under strain: illness, short sleep, heavy training or stress. It often moves before you feel anything.
Blood oxygen	%	Wearable	Yes	A sustained drop can point to a breathing or lung problem or a cabin atmosphere issue.
Body temperature	°C	Wearable	Yes	A rise above your own normal supports the picture of infection or inflammation; body temperature also runs differently in space.
Sleep duration	h	Wearable	Yes	Crews sleep less in space than on Earth. Short sleep builds up as a debt that slows reactions and raises error risk.
Sleep timing shift	h	Wearable	Yes	When sleep slides away from the schedule, your body clock and the mission clock disagree, which hurts sleep quality and alertness.
Exercise, 7-day average	min/day	Wearable	Yes	Daily exercise is the main protection against losing bone, muscle and heart fitness without gravity. Missed sessions add up quietly.
Reaction time	ms	Daily check-in	Yes	Reaction time is a sensitive, objective sign of fatigue. It slows with sleep loss and may slow with high cabin carbon dioxide.
Mood	/5	Daily check-in	Yes	Isolation, confinement and distance from home wear on everyone. A slow slide is easier to turn around when it is noticed early.
Fluid intake	L	Daily check-in	Yes	Crews tend to drink too little in space. Low intake raises the risk of kidney stones and makes the heart work harder.
Food intake	% of plan	Daily check-in	Yes	Under-eating is common in space and speeds up loss of body mass, muscle and bone.
Body mass	kg	Weekly measurement	Shown only	Steady loss of body mass signals under-eating or dehydration.
Blood pressure (upper)	mmHg	Weekly measurement	Shown only	Blood pressure regulation changes without gravity. Readings well outside your normal deserve a repeat and a word with the medical officer.
Blood sugar	mg/dL	Weekly measurement	Shown only	Named in the challenge as a useful indicator. Keel shows it against your own previous readings and a general reference range.
Cabin carbon dioxide	mmHg	Cabin sensor	Own rule	In a closed cabin, exhaled carbon dioxide builds up. Higher levels are linked to headaches and may dull thinking.
Radiation dose	mSv/day	Cabin sensor	Own rule	Beyond Earth's magnetic shield the crew receives a steady dose from cosmic rays and occasional bursts from solar storms. The total is tracked against a career limit.

Bone loss and eye changes (SANS) cannot be measured daily with small hardware. Keel tracks the countermeasure instead (exercise done) and asks about vision at check-in.

Data and adapters
Real open data. Stanford COVID-19 wearables, phases 1 and 2 (heart rate, steps, dated illness): detection testing. LifeSnaps (Fitbit Sense, 71 people): day-to-day variability. NASA Life Sciences Data Archive public bed-rest campaigns (daily vitals of 29 volunteers): gravity-transition handling.
Your own files. CSV, Fitbit export JSON, Apple Health export. Parsed in the browser, never uploaded.
A live heart-rate strap over Web Bluetooth (standard heart-rate service), giving a resting heart rate and HRV reading.
The simulator, for everything a real dataset does not exist for. Simulated numbers are always labelled.

There is no open astronaut wearable time series. NASA's in-flight actigraphy, cognition and standard-measures data are available on request only; we did not request them.

Daily features from raw wearable data: resting heart rate is the median heart rate between midnight and 07:00 over minutes with no steps in the previous 12 minutes (at least 30 such minutes); steps are the daily total on days with 10 hours of wear; sleep is hours in non-wake stages.

The simulator

Each simulated crew member has personal set points. Each day's reading is the set point, plus a shift for the mission phase, plus the effect of hidden drivers (sleep debt, exercise load, an illness, a cabin CO2 rise, low fluid intake, a low-mood period), plus day-to-day noise that carries over partly to the next day.

x[i,t] = set_point[i] + flight_shift[i] * a(t) + SUM_k beta_k * driver_k[i,t] + noise[i,t]
a(t)   = 1 - exp(-(t - launch) / tau)                        adaptation to weightlessness
noise  = rho * noise[t-1] + e,   e ~ N(0, sd_i^2 (1 - rho^2))   sd_i differs between people

Of 64 parameters, 17 are taken from published sources, 12 are fitted to real data (one half of the people; the other half checks the result on the Validation screen), and 35 are our assumptions. The illness response in heart rate and the day-to-day noise are fitted to real data. Effect sizes for HRV, temperature, oxygen, mood, reaction time, and every non-illness event are assumptions.

Personal baselines

For each person and indicator, "normal" is the median of their own last 28 days, leaving out the most recent 3 so that a change that is just starting cannot hide inside its own baseline. Spread is the median absolute deviation, which a few bad sensor readings cannot inflate. A baseline needs 7 valid days.

m[t] = median( x[t-3-28 .. t-3-1] )
s[t] = max( floor, 1.4826 * MAD( same window ) )
z[t] = clip( (x[t] - m[t]) / s[t], -4, +4 )

Time of day and activity. Daily features are built so these cancel out: resting heart rate uses only still minutes at night, HRV is overnight. Adjusting heart rate for the previous day's activity was tested on real data and did not help, so it is switched off.

Mission phase. At launch or landing the body changes on purpose. The window never looks back across a phase change. Until 14 days of new data exist, the baseline is a blend of the old baseline moved along the expected adaptation curve and the person's own new data, and the band is widened:

prior[t] = m_before + shift * (1 - exp(-(t - t0)/tau))
w        = min(1, n_new / 14)
m[t]     = (1 - w) * prior[t] + w * median(new data)
s[t]     = (1 - w) * s_before * (1 + 1.5 * exp(-(t - t0)/tau)) + w * s_new

On NASA bed-rest data this roughly halves false flags when volunteers get up after weeks in bed; in simulation it halves false alerts in the first month of flight (Validation, sections 2 and 4).

Detection

Four ways of turning daily z-scores into an alert were compared with a simple baseline that uses one threshold for everybody:

Fixed threshold (comparison): population mean and spread, alert when 2 of 3 days exceed it.
Personal z-score: the same 2-of-3 rule on the person's own z.
CUSUM (change-point): adds up evidence day by day, S[t] = max(0, S[t-1] + z[t] - 0.75), and resets after 3 normal days.
Multivariate EWMA: smooths all indicators together and asks how unusual the combination is, so several small changes in a worrying direction count.
Fusion: whichever of the last two is closer to its threshold.
y[t] = 0.3 * z[t] + 0.7 * y[t-1]            (a missing reading counts as 0)
y+   = y clipped to the direction of concern (e.g. only high heart rate, only low HRV)
T2   = y+' * inverse( 0.3/(2-0.3) * R ) * y+          R = correlation of z between indicators

The app runs the multivariate EWMA. That choice was made on simulated training missions only; test missions and real data had no vote.

Alarm fatigue is a hazard, so thresholds are set from a false-alert budget. A notice may fire at most 1 time per person-month by mistake; an alert, which is the only tier that can recommend involving the medical officer without a reported symptom, at most 0.25. An alert that stays open counts again every 7 days, so one endless alert cannot hide inside the budget. Runs separated by 3 days or fewer are one alert.

Cabin CO2, radiation and red-flag symptoms do not go through the detector: they have their own fixed rules, because they are not about a personal baseline.

How it was tested
Held-out people. Real data: five-fold cross-validation over people. Population statistics, correlations and thresholds come from the training folds; each person is scored once by a model that never saw them. Simulation: separate training and test missions.
Detection. An episode counts as detected if an alert starts between 10 days before and 4 days after onset. Lead time is onset minus alert start.
False alerts. Alerts that start more than 21 days before or 28 days after any labeled onset, per person-month of such quiet time.
Luck. At one false alert per month an alert lands in a two-week window by chance fairly often. We place pretend events on random quiet days and report that rate next to every detection rate.
Intervals. Bootstrap over people (real) or missions (simulated), 95%.
Leakage checks. Baselines only look backwards (unit-tested). Thresholds never see test people (unit-tested). The simulator is calibrated on one half of the real people and checked against the other. The deployed detector was picked on training missions.

From alert to action

When the detector has an alert open, Keel looks at which indicators moved over the last three days and walks down a fixed table of 14 rules. The first rule that matches supplies the wording, the single recommended action, the follow-up steps and the escalation level. Each rule cites a countermeasure with its source. No language model writes or rephrases any of it.

Escalation has three levels: manage it yourself; inform the crew medical officer; contact the ground when a link is available. A rule can raise the level when a pattern persists. Going beyond self-management needs either the stricter alert tier or a symptom the crew member reported, so a single noisy night cannot summon the medical officer.

Crew readiness and privacy

The commander sees one of three readiness tiers per person for a demanding task tomorrow, derived from open alerts, reaction time and recent sleep. The tier is always visible; the reasons are visible only where the crew member has shared that area. Crew-wide patterns are shown as counts, and only when at least two people are affected.

Sharing is set per role and per area at four levels: private, status, trends, full detail. One function decides what any other role may see, and it is unit-tested to leak nothing that was not shared. Every view by another role is written to the crew member's access log. Cabin readings are not personal and are visible to all.

The summary queued for the ground contains only what the crew member shares with the flight surgeon, and is a few hundred bytes.

Limitations
Ground data stands in for spaceflight data. The people in the real evaluation were on Earth, wore consumer watches and mostly had COVID-19. Astronauts are fitter, live on a schedule and are in a different physiological state.
Early warning is not demonstrated. On real data the alert usually arrives on or after the day symptoms start.
Small samples. About a hundred illness episodes; 29 bed-rest volunteers; 71 people for variability.
Only illness is tested on real data. Sleep loss, CO2, deconditioning, dehydration and low mood are tested in simulation with assumed effect sizes.
Sensors differ. Overnight heart rate from a wrist watch varies by about 3.5 bpm from night to night; flight hardware may be better or worse. Apple Watch data in the real set performed poorly.
What is simulated: every mission in the app, the cabin CO2 and radiation readings, the link delay and blackouts, and thresholds for the full indicator set.
Assumed, not verified: the size of in-flight changes in heart rate and HRV (sources say "unchanged" or disagree), typical cabin CO2, crewed-mission blackout lengths.
The readiness tier and the action rules have not been reviewed by a clinician. Thresholds such as 38.0 °C or 92% oxygen are general public guidance, not flight rules.
The one-minute reaction test is shorter than the validated three-minute test and depends on the touchscreen.

What a clinician would need to review before any real use: the rule table and its wording; escalation thresholds; which red-flag symptoms bypass the detector; the false-alert budgets; behaviour during medication use; and whether personal baselines might normalise a slow, harmful drift.
