The idea

On the International Space Station a flight surgeon is seconds away by voice, speaks privately with each crew member about once a week, and the ground team follows exercise and cabin-air data. On the way to Mars the signal takes minutes each way and sometimes there is no link at all, so the crew has to notice and act on health changes themselves.

Keel learns what is normal for each person, notices when their readings drift away from that, says what changed in plain words, and points to one documented action. All of it runs on the device. The crew member owns the data and chooses what the medical officer, the commander and the ground can see.

Keel is decision support, not a medical device. It never states a diagnosis. It says "this is unusual for you" and "this is what the documented countermeasure is".

Limitations
Ground data stands in for spaceflight data. The people in the real evaluation were on Earth, wore consumer watches and mostly had COVID-19. Astronauts are fitter, live on a schedule and are in a different physiological state.
Early warning is not demonstrated. On real data the alert usually starts on or after the day symptoms begin.
Most alerts are false. At in-flight illness rates only about one alert-tier alert in ten would be a real respiratory illness. Keel is a prompt to look, not a verdict.
Small samples. About a hundred illness episodes; 29 bed-rest volunteers; 71 people for variability.
Only illness is tested on real data, and most of that signal comes from people moving less once they feel ill. Sleep loss, CO2, deconditioning, dehydration and low mood are tested in simulation with assumed effect sizes.
Sensors differ. Overnight heart rate from a wrist watch varies by about 3.5 bpm from night to night; flight hardware may be better or worse. Non-Fitbit data in the real set performed poorly. Wearable sleep detection is not validated in weightlessness.
Thresholds for the full sensor set are calibrated in simulation. When simulated thresholds for two indicators were applied to real people, false alerts ran about 40 to 70% over budget. Expect the same or worse for the full set.
Handling of gravity changes has a blind spot: for the first weeks after launch or landing the widened band hides real problems.
A personal baseline can normalise a slow, harmful drift. Showing the standard next to "your usual" and comparing body mass with pre-flight only partly addresses this.
Not modelled: sleep aids, anti-nausea and other medication; menstrual-cycle effects on temperature and heart rate; body temperature running higher on long flights, which a reviewer pointed out and which would make a fixed 38.0 °C line too low or too high.
What is simulated: every mission in the app, the cabin CO2 and radiation readings, the link delay and blackouts.
Assumed, not verified: the size of in-flight changes in heart rate and HRV (sources say "unchanged" or disagree), typical cabin CO2, crewed-mission blackout lengths.
No clinician has reviewed the rules, the fixed thresholds or the readiness tier. The thresholds are general public guidance for adults on Earth, not flight rules.
The one-minute reaction test is shorter than the validated three-minute test and depends on the touchscreen.

What a clinician would need to review before any real use: every rule's wording, urgency and audience; each fixed threshold; the red-flag list; the false-alert budgets; what emergency sharing covers; behaviour during medication use; and how long the band stays widened after a gravity change.
