The idea

On the International Space Station a flight surgeon is seconds away by voice, speaks privately with each crew member about once a week, and the ground team follows exercise and cabin-air data. On the way to Mars the signal takes minutes each way and sometimes there is no link at all, so the crew has to notice and act on health changes themselves.

Keel learns what is normal for each person, notices when their readings drift away from that, says what changed in plain words, and points to one documented action. All of it runs on the device. The crew member owns the data and chooses most of what the medical officer, the commander and the ground can see (always shared: a readiness tier for the commander and, under emergency sharing, urgent safety items).

Keel is decision support, not a medical device. It never states a diagnosis. It says "this is unusual for you" and "this is what the documented countermeasure is".

Limitations
Ground data stands in for spaceflight data. The people in the real evaluation were on Earth, wore consumer watches and mostly had COVID-19. Astronauts are fitter, live on a schedule and are in a different physiological state.
Useful early warning is not demonstrated. At the notice tier somewhat more alerts start before symptom onset than luck would give, but the gain is small and confounded by testing and isolation, and it counts the day of onset itself. At the alert tier it is about half as large, with an interval that includes zero for the two-sensor linking analysis and small for heart rate alone, the model imports run on (Validation, section 1).
Most alerts are false. At in-flight illness rates for the import model about one in 16 to one in 20 alert-tier alerts, and one in 67 to 70 notices, would be a real respiratory illness (these use the narrower detection window; in the window planned first, notices are about 1 in 94, and the alert tier was not computed there) (likely optimistic, if false-alert rates hold as measured on Earth); for the tablet's own model on real data it is not measured; the shipped model file fed only import channels gives about 1 in 16 alert-tier alerts and 1 in 29 notices, the closest available figure, though flattered: on those channels it under-alerts (0.12 and 0.35 false alerts a month against budgets of 0.25 and 1.00); at its budgets it would be about 1 in 32 and 1 in 79, and with more sensors likely worse. Keel is a prompt to look, not a verdict.
Small samples. About a hundred illness episodes; 29 bed-rest volunteers, 14 or 15 per transition; 10 to 73 people per indicator in the half used to check variability.
The alert tier sits close to a ceiling. The 2-of-3 score uses z-scores capped at 4 and the alert threshold is about 3.5, so with some sensor sets the alert tier cannot fire at all (it never fired for heart rate with 7-day activity on real data, nor in simulation without phase handling). A flight version would score on uncapped values or raise the cap.
The on-board model's simulated result barely depends on heart rate (removing it changes overall detection by about a point); it rests on sleep, exercise and check-in channels whose effects in the simulator are assumed.
Only illness is tested on real data, and much of that signal (about a third above luck, judged from two separate runs; the paired difference was not estimated) may come with people moving less around illness, which cannot be separated from isolating at home. Sleep loss, CO2, deconditioning, dehydration and low mood are tested in simulation with assumed effect sizes.
Sensors differ. Overnight heart rate from a wrist watch varies by about 3.3 to 3.5 bpm from night to night; flight hardware may be better or worse. Non-Fitbit data in the real set did worse, in a small sample. Wearable sleep detection is not validated in weightlessness.
Thresholds for the full sensor set are calibrated in simulation. When simulated thresholds were applied to real people, false alerts ran about 30% over budget at the notice tier and 45% over at the alert tier with two indicators on the 72 quiet people from the half the simulator was not fitted on (on all 251 people, 31% and 64% over), and about 34% and 134% over with five (16 people, 29 person-months, no interval; LifeSnaps has no illness labels, so every alert there counts as false). The full set would need recalibrating on real crew data.
Handling of gravity changes showed no measured cost for events right after launch once luck was taken from the same flight days, but the app's version was tested only in simulation (bed-rest data tested a simpler pulse-only version), where the size of the adaptation is assumed.
Thresholds are set per sensor set only twice: the full on-board set (from simulation) and overnight heart rate alone (from real people, used for imports). Any other combination, such as a crew member whose HRV sensor fails, runs on the full-set thresholds; in simulation it then alerts less than its budget, which may not hold on real data.
A personal baseline can normalise a slow, harmful drift. Showing the standard next to "your usual" and comparing body mass with pre-flight only partly addresses this.
Not modelled: sleep aids, anti-nausea and other medication; menstrual-cycle effects on temperature and heart rate; resting core temperature, which one ISS study found had risen about 1 °C over the first two and a half months [68], so the medical officer should judge a thermometer reading against the person's own in-flight readings; respiratory rate, the strongest wearable illness signal in one of our sources.
What is simulated: every mission in the app, the cabin CO2 and radiation readings, the link delay and blackouts.
Assumed, not verified: the size of in-flight changes in heart rate and HRV (sources say "unchanged" or disagree), typical cabin CO2, crewed-mission blackout lengths.
No clinician has reviewed the rules, the fixed thresholds or the readiness tier. Some thresholds come from general public guidance for adults on Earth and others (heart rate 35, 120 and 150, and the severe blood-pressure and blood-sugar lines) are provisional values we chose; none are flight rules.
The one-minute reaction test is shorter than the validated three-minute test and depends on the touchscreen.

What a clinician would need to review before any real use: every rule's wording, urgency and audience; each fixed threshold; the red-flag list; the false-alert budgets; what emergency sharing covers; behaviour during medication use; and how long the band stays widened after a gravity change.
