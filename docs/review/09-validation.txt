Does it work?

Every number here was produced by a script in the repository. Real data is used wherever it exists; everything else is simulated and labelled. Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals over people (real data) or missions (simulation), with thresholds held fixed, which makes them somewhat narrower than they should be. Analysis choices such as the metric, the detection window and the detector selection rule were revised during several of the review rounds, so this is not a pre-planned analysis; the decision log in the repository records each change.

The short version
By design: with no link to Earth, it answers a dangerous reading or a serious symptom at once with one sourced step and an alert to the medical officer and commander. Unit-tested; no clinician has reviewed the thresholds.
On real illness: heart rate alone is +13 points above chance (4 to 24) in the detection window set before the analysis, and +19 (9 to 28; 50% of 107 episodes against 31% by chance) in the narrower window we moved to, at about one false notice a month. No early warning was shown, and most alerts are false. Imported watch data runs on this model.
Everything else is simulated, including the tablet's own eleven-channel model (simulated illness +14, -6 to 34). The tiles and sections below are detail.

What these results do not show. Useful early warning. At the notice tier an alert started in the ten days before onset or on the day itself for 44% of episodes, against 30% by luck (+14, 4 to 24); at the alert tier, the one that brings in the medical officer, +6 (-1 to 13), about nothing. Those figures are for the two-sensor linking analysis; for heart rate alone, the model imports run on, they are +14 (5 to 23) and +7 (0 to 14). Of these four intervals, 3 exclude zero (an interval whose lower end is just above zero can still show as 0 after rounding). They count the day of onset itself; a luck-adjusted figure for alerts starting strictly before onset was not computed, and onset dates lean on test dates. The notice-tier gain is small and confounded (test dates, isolation after a positive test, lower activity on the day itself), so we do not claim early warning. Most alerts are false: at the in-flight rate of respiratory illness, about 1 in 20 alert-tier alerts and about 1 in 70 notices would be a real illness for the import model on the watch maker's own resting heart rate, as imports carry it, assuming detection carries over unchanged (not measured) (about 1 in 16 alert-tier alerts on night-time heart rate), and about 1 in 22 for the two-sensor version (a spread across configurations, not an interval; all likely optimistic). The model file the app ships is calibrated for all eleven channels: fed only overnight heart rate (and sleep where recorded), as a real import would be, it flagged 29% of episodes (+16 above luck) at 0.35 false alerts per person-month, and at its alert tier it flagged 14% at 0.12 false alerts per person-month, against a budget of 0.25. Thresholds have to be set for the sensors a person actually has, so imported watch data now runs on a separate heart-rate-only model calibrated on these real people: the "heart rate alone" row below (+19, 9 to 28). Keel runs a simple personal rule, and the more complex detectors are not shown to beat it, and its lead over one fixed threshold depends on choices we made after seeing data. The data is from people on Earth with consumer watches, mostly with COVID-19. Everything except illness is tested in simulation only.

How much depends on how luck is measured

Luck is estimated by placing pretend illness dates on quiet days and scoring them like real ones. Pretend dates handle missing data the same way as real ones (they carry the person's quiet-time gaps, not the extra unworn days illness brings) and are weighted to the device mix of the real episodes: luck 34%, gap +28. Matched to how far into the recording real onsets fall (a young baseline is noisier): 33%, gap +30. Using only people who had an episode: 33%, gap +30. Requiring a score on every day of a pretend window, as our earlier analysis did: 35%, gap +27. As a share of what luck leaves to be found, the gap is 43%.

What this cannot fix. Pretend events are placed on label-quiet days with the same missing data as real windows, and weighted to the device mix of the real episodes. They cannot be matched to the calendar (dates are shifted per person), so exposure, travel, testing and isolation near a real onset are not reproduced; detection above luck is therefore likely optimistic on what physiology alone explains.

What an alert is worth

ISS crews report about one episode of upper-respiratory symptoms per person-year. At that rate, and with the detection above luck measured here, about 1 in 13 alert-tier alerts and about 1 in 44 notices would be attributable to such an illness with daily steps and thresholds from real people; none (that tier never fires) and about 1 in 42 with the 7-day activity average; and about 1 in 22 and about 1 in 60 with thresholds from the simulator, which run over budget; on the watch maker's own resting heart rate, where the import model's false-alert rates are higher (assuming detection carries over unchanged, which was not measured), about 1 in 20 and about 1 in 70; and about 1 in 16 and about 1 in 67 for the heart-rate-only model imports run on. These are likely optimistic estimates of the share that is a respiratory illness (they assume one true alert per illness and false-alert rates measured on Earth): the sensitivity comes from mostly symptomatic COVID-19, in-flight colds are milder, and full-sensor thresholds running over budget (section 3) would lower them further. Alerts for the other conditions Keel watches for are not counted here; they cannot be measured on real data. For imported watch data the closest figures are the heart-rate-only ones on the watch maker's resting heart rate; for the on-board model, the simulator-threshold ones. This is why a notice asks for a look, not for the medical officer.

Uses the narrower detection window; with the pre-set window's heart-rate-alone detection, notices would be about 1 in 94 (computed here from the same result file; the false-alert rate does not depend on the window). False alerts over long quiet periods

People with no dated illness in their recording (146 people, 452 scored person-months; many were never confirmed healthy, so some alerts may be real). Notice tier: 0.95 false alerts per person-month (interval 0.85 to 1.08), with an alert open on 9% of quiet days. Alert tier: 0.23 per person-month, open on 2% of days; 61% of people never saw one. The noisiest fifth of people produced 74% of alert-tier false alerts, against 60% if everyone had the same rate: more concentrated than chance, so a few people carry much of the burden. So at the alert tier some individuals may be systematically noisier; at the notice tier the share matches a constant rate (44% against 44%). No formal test. Between groups (computed over all people, not only this group) the rates look different, though the intervals overlap: non-Fitbit devices 0.78 per person-month (0.60 to 0.95) against 1.02 for Fitbit, and 1.08 for phase 1 people who reported no illness against 0.89 for phase 2 people not in the positives table.

Not counted anywhere: alerts starting 11 to 21 days before an episode or 5 to 28 days after it fall outside the detection window but inside the exclusion zone. There were 74 at the notice tier (0.7 per episode) and 29 at the alert tier, so the false-alert rates understate the burden for people who were ill.

Steps may add much of the signal, and that is a caution (the paired difference was not estimated). Heart rate alone is +19 points above luck; with steps, +28. Median daily steps fell from 7,554 to 4,210 in the five days from onset (89 episodes): people rest, or isolate after a positive test. A crew on a fixed exercise schedule may not show that, and because the data owners shifted every person's dates we cannot separate it from lockdowns.
At the alert tier, heart rate with the 7-day activity average could not be calibrated. With daily steps Keel is +26 above luck. With the smoother 7-day average the alert-tier threshold lands on the cap of the 2-of-3 score (z-scores are clipped at 4), so that tier never fires: this is a limit of the rule's calibration, not evidence about the activity feature, and a flight version would need ties at the cap broken or a higher cap. At the notice tier its lead over a fixed threshold is +23 (11 to 35).
Adjusting heart rate for the previous day's activity does not help (+27 against +28), so the app leaves it off.
Sleep is undecided. Too few people have sleep data.
Device may matter (no interval for the difference). Fitbit: 67% flagged (+33 above luck, 89 episodes). Non-Fitbit devices: 39% (+7, 18 episodes, all Apple Watch); their overnight heart-rate samples are sparse in this release.

It holds across cohorts. Trained on phase 1 and tested on phase 2: 61% flagged, +29 above luck, 0.92 false alerts per person-month. The reverse: 61%, +22, 1.04. Five different fold splits gave +26 to +30 above luck.
Retrospective and prospective phase 2 cases (the prospective ones were enrolled under the original study's own alerting): +37 and +25 above luck.
The window. -14..+7 days: 74% flagged, 45% by luck, gap +29 (20 to 38); -10..+4 days: 63% flagged, 34% by luck, gap +28 (19 to 38); -7..+0 days: 32% flagged, 22% by luck, gap +10 (0 to 20); -3..+0 days: 21% flagged, 10% by luck, gap +12 (4 to 20). The window was narrowed from −14..+7 to −10..+4 while the real-data evaluation was being written. The commit history cannot show that this came before the first run, so the original window's figures are given beside the main ones. For this configuration the two windows give nearly the same gap; for heart rate alone, the figure the import model rests on, the original window gives +13 (4 to 24) against +19 in the narrower one, and that lower figure is the one in the originally planned window (only the window was planned beforehand).
Published comparison. The dataset's authors reported 63% of 32 cases detectable before symptoms with about 0.66 alarms per month, using hourly data and their own algorithm on phase 1. We did not reproduce early detection with daily features.

Limits you should know before trusting any of this
No open dataset has astronaut wearable time series. The real evaluation uses people on Earth; spaceflight physiology is represented only by published summary values and the bed-rest analog.
The real illness data has two indicators. Claims about HRV, oxygen, temperature, mood, reaction time, fluids and food rest on simulation with assumed effect sizes.
107 real episodes is a small sample; intervals are wide. Symptom dates are self-reported and shifted for privacy.
Thresholds for the full indicator set are calibrated on simulated missions and probably run over budget on real people.
Detection above luck is likely optimistic about what physiology explains: pretend dates cannot be matched to the calendar, so travel, testing and isolation around a real onset are not reproduced. Heart rate alone (+13 in the pre-set window, +19 in the narrower one) is the safer figure.
The simulator's illness response was fitted on 38 episode curves from 55 people; 47 of the 107 evaluated episodes belong to those people, so anything that passes through the simulator (the app's thresholds, the choice of detector) has seen those people; results on the 132 people not used by its fits are given beside the headline (not unseen: the 30% exercise cut and the 30% chance of an unworn night in simulated ground people were set with the real data in view).
The four personal-baseline detectors perform alike at the notice tier. Keel runs the one picked on simulated training missions by a selection rule that was itself revised during review; the choice has flipped twice, most recently to the simple rule after a sign error in the multivariate statistic was corrected. The simple rule is +28 above luck at the notice tier and +26 at the alert tier.
