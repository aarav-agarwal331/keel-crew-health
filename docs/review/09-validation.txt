Does it work?

Every number here was produced by a script in the repository. Real data is used wherever it exists; everything else is simulated and labelled. Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals over people. No fitted quantity (threshold, correlation, population statistic) saw the people it was tested on. Analysis choices such as the metric and the detector selection rule were revised during two review rounds; the decision log in the repository records each one.

The short version

above luck, on real illness
Keel flagged 66% of 107 real illness episodes at one false alert per person-month. Luck alone gives 36%. Interval for the gap: 20 to 40 points. With heart rate alone, without steps: +17 (7 to 27).
+17 points
for one threshold for everyone
A fixed threshold flagged 41%, with 25% by luck. Keel's advantage: +14 points (interval 0 to 28).
0.99 to 1.38
false alerts per person-month, budget 1.00
0.99 when thresholds come from other real people (close to budget by design). 1.38 when they come from simulation only, as the app's full-sensor thresholds do. Alert tier (budget 0.25): 0.25 and 0.39; there Keel is +32 above luck with daily steps but only +12 with the 7-day activity average the app itself uses.
What these results do not show. Keel does not give early warning: on real data the alert mostly starts on or after the day symptoms begin (39% of episodes had an alert start in the ten days up to onset; luck gives 29%). Most alerts are false: at the in-flight rate of respiratory illness, between about 1 in 11 and about 1 in 28 alert-tier alerts would be a real illness. The data is from people on Earth with consumer watches. Everything except illness is tested in simulation only.

What an alert is worth

ISS crews report about one episode of upper-respiratory symptoms per person-year. At that rate, and with the detection above luck measured here, about 1 in 11 alert-tier alerts and about 1 in 41 notices would coincide with such an illness when daily steps are used, and about 1 in 28 and about 1 in 46 with the 7-day activity average the app uses. The other conditions Keel watches for would raise those shares, by an amount we cannot measure on real data. This is why a notice asks for a look, not for the medical officer.

Assumes 0.97 illnesses per person-year (upper-respiratory symptoms on the ISS, source 52b) and the detection above luck measured on real data. How this was measured · research/scripts/eval_real.py

False alerts over long quiet periods

People with no dated illness in their recording (146 people, 452 scored person-months; many were never confirmed healthy, so some alerts may be real). Notice tier: 0.99 false alerts per person-month (interval 0.88 to 1.10), with an alert open on 10% of quiet days. Alert tier: 0.24 per person-month, open on 2% of days; 54% of people never saw one. The noisiest fifth of people produced 63% of alert-tier false alerts, which is what pure chance would give (59%): we found no evidence that some people are systematically noisier.

Steps add much of the signal, and that is a caution. Heart rate alone is +17 points above luck; with steps, +30. Median daily steps fell from 7,554 to 4,210 in the five days from onset (89 episodes): people rest, or isolate after a positive test. A crew on a fixed exercise schedule may not show that, and because the data owners shifted every person's dates we cannot separate it from lockdowns.
At the alert tier the choice of activity feature matters. With daily steps Keel is +32 above luck; with the smoother 7-day average the app uses, +12.
Adjusting heart rate for the previous day's activity does not help (+32 against +30), so the app leaves it off.
Sleep is undecided. Too few people have sleep data.
Device matters. Fitbit: 72% flagged (+35 above luck, 89 episodes). Non-Fitbit devices: 39% (+9, 18 episodes, all Apple Watch); their overnight heart-rate samples are sparse in this release.
People who reported no illness in phase 1 saw 1.17 false alerts per person-month at the notice tier (interval 0.96 to 1.37).
It holds across cohorts. Trained on phase 1 and tested on phase 2: 62% flagged, +29 above luck, 0.87 false alerts per person-month. The reverse: 71%, +36, 1.11. Five different fold splits gave +30 to +32 above luck.
Retrospective and prospective phase 2 cases (the prospective ones were enrolled under the original study's own alerting): +29 and +27 above luck.
The window. -14..+7 days: 73% flagged, 46% by luck; -10..+4 days: 66% flagged, 36% by luck; -7..+0 days: 34% flagged, 21% by luck; -3..+0 days: 21% flagged, 12% by luck. The window was narrowed from −14..+7 to −10..+4 while the real-data evaluation was being written. The commit history cannot show that this came before the first run, and −10..+4 has the largest gap of the four, so treat +27 for the original window as the conservative figure.
Published comparison. The dataset's authors reported 63% of 32 cases detectable before symptoms with about 0.66 alarms per month, using hourly data and their own algorithm on phase 1. We did not reproduce early detection with daily features.

Limits you should know before trusting any of this
No open dataset has astronaut wearable time series. The real evaluation uses people on Earth; spaceflight physiology is represented only by published summary values and the bed-rest analog.
The real illness data has two indicators. Claims about HRV, oxygen, temperature, mood, reaction time, fluids and food rest on simulation with assumed effect sizes.
107 real episodes is a small sample; intervals are wide. Symptom dates are self-reported and shifted for privacy.
Thresholds for the full indicator set are calibrated on simulated missions and probably run over budget on real people.
The four personal-baseline detectors perform alike at the notice tier. Keel runs the one picked on simulated training missions by a selection rule that was itself revised during review; the choice flipped once. The simple rule is +27 above luck at the notice tier and +25 at the alert tier.
