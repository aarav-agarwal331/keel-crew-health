Does it work?

Every number here was produced by a script in the repository. Real data is used wherever it exists; everything else is simulated and labelled. Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals over people. Nothing was tuned on the people it was tested on.

The short version
+31 points
above luck, on real illness
Keel flagged 67% of 103 real illness episodes at one false alert per person-month. Luck alone gives 36%. Interval for the gap: 21 to 41 points.
+15 points
for one threshold for everyone
A fixed threshold flagged 40%, with 24% by luck. Keel's advantage: +15 points (interval 2 to 30).
1.00
false alerts per person-month
Measured on held-out people against a budget of 1.00. At the stricter alert tier: 0.24 against 0.25, and +31 points above luck.
What these results do not show. Keel does not give early warning: on real data the alert mostly starts on or after the day symptoms begin (40% of episodes had an alert start in the ten days up to onset; luck gives 28%). Most alerts are false: at the in-flight rate of respiratory illness, about 1 in 11 alert-tier alerts would be a real illness. The data is from people on Earth with consumer watches. Everything except illness is tested in simulation only.

What an alert is worth

ISS crews report about one episode of upper-respiratory symptoms per person-year. At that rate, and with the detection above luck measured here, about 1 in 11 alert-tier alerts and about 1 in 41 notices would coincide with such an illness. The other conditions Keel watches for would raise those shares, by an amount we cannot measure on real data. This is why a notice asks for a look, not for the medical officer.

Illness rate 0.97 per person-year (upper-respiratory symptoms on the ISS, DOMAIN ref 52b); sensitivity = detection net of luck on real data. How this was measured · research/scripts/eval_real.py

False alerts over long quiet periods

People with no dated illness in their recording (150 people, 459 scored person-months; many were never confirmed healthy, so some alerts may be real). Notice tier: 0.99 false alerts per person-month (interval 0.90 to 1.10), with an alert open on 10% of quiet days. Alert tier: 0.23 per person-month, open on 2% of days; 56% of people never saw one. The noisiest fifth of people produced 63% of alert-tier false alerts, which is what pure chance would give (59%): we found no evidence that some people are systematically noisier.

Steps add most of the signal, and that is a caution. Heart rate alone is +14 points above luck; with steps, +31. People's steps halve once they feel ill and stay home. A crew on a fixed exercise schedule may not show that.
Adjusting heart rate for the previous day's activity does not help (+31 against +31), so the app leaves it off.
Sleep is undecided. Too few people have sleep data.
Device matters. Fitbit: 73% flagged (+36 above luck, 85 episodes). Other devices, mostly Apple Watch: 39% (+8, 18 episodes); their overnight heart-rate samples are sparse in this release.
It holds across cohorts. Trained on phase 1 and tested on phase 2: 62% flagged, +29 above luck, 0.87 false alerts per person-month. The reverse: 73%, +38, 1.10. Five different fold splits gave +31 to +33 above luck.
Retrospective and prospective phase 2 cases (the prospective ones were enrolled under the original study's own alerting): +31 and +25 above luck.
The window. -14..+7 days: 74% flagged, 49% by luck; -10..+4 days: 67% flagged, 36% by luck; -7..+0 days: 34% flagged, 22% by luck; -3..+0 days: 21% flagged, 11% by luck. The −10 to +4 window was fixed before the first run on real data; it also turned out to have the largest gap.
Published comparison. The dataset's authors reported 63% of 32 cases detectable before symptoms with about 0.66 alarms per month, using hourly data and their own algorithm on phase 1. We did not reproduce early detection with daily features.

Limits you should know before trusting any of this
No open dataset has astronaut wearable time series. The real evaluation uses people on Earth; spaceflight physiology is represented only by published summary values and the bed-rest analog.
The real illness data has two indicators. Claims about HRV, oxygen, temperature, mood, reaction time, fluids and food rest on simulation with assumed effect sizes.
103 real episodes is a small sample; intervals are wide. Symptom dates are self-reported and shifted for privacy.
Thresholds for the full indicator set are calibrated on simulated missions and probably run over budget on real people.
The four personal-baseline detectors perform alike. Keel runs the one a pre-set rule picked on simulated training missions.
