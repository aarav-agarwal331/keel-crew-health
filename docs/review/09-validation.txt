Does it work?

Every number here was produced by a script in the repository. Real data is used wherever it exists; everything else is simulated and labelled. Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals over people, with thresholds held fixed. Analysis choices such as the metric, the detection window and the detector selection rule were revised during three review rounds, so this is not a pre-planned analysis; the decision log in the repository records each change.

The short version
+32 points
above luck on real illness, as the app runs
With thresholds taken from the simulator, as in the app, and its two real-data sensors (overnight heart rate, 7-day activity), Keel flagged 72% of 107 real illness episodes. Luck alone gives 40%. Interval for the gap: 22 to 41. On the 113 people nothing was fitted on (41 episodes): +38 (23 to 50).
1.40 a month
false alerts per person, against a budget of 1.00
Simulator-set thresholds run over budget on real people. With thresholds set on other real people the rate is 1.00 and the gap above luck is +31 (21 to 41). A flight system would have to be recalibrated on real crew data.
+19 points
at the stricter tier that brings in the medical officer
32% flagged, 13% by luck, interval 9 to 28, at 0.39 false alerts per person-month (budget 0.25). On people nothing was fitted on: +23 (8 to 38). With thresholds from other real people: +13 (4 to 21).
What these results do not show. Keel does not give early warning: on real data the alert mostly starts on or after the day symptoms begin (39% of episodes had an alert start in the ten days up to onset; luck gives 24%). Most alerts are false: at the in-flight rate of respiratory illness, between about 1 in 11 and about 1 in 25 alert-tier alerts would be a real illness. Keel is not shown to beat a simple personal rule, and its lead over one fixed threshold depends on choices we made after seeing data. The data is from people on Earth with consumer watches, mostly with COVID-19. Everything except illness is tested in simulation only.

How much depends on how luck is measured

Luck is estimated by placing pretend illness dates on quiet days and scoring them like real ones. Pretend dates carry the same missing data as real ones and are weighted to the device mix of the real episodes: luck 33%, gap +34. Using only people who had an episode: 32%, gap +34. Requiring a score on every day of a pretend window, as our earlier analysis did: 36%, gap +30. As a share of what luck leaves to be found, the gap is 50%.

What this cannot fix. Pretend events are placed on label-quiet days with the same missing data as real windows, and weighted to the device mix of the real episodes. They cannot be matched to the calendar (dates are shifted per person), so exposure, travel, testing and isolation near a real onset are not reproduced; detection above luck is therefore an upper bound on what physiology alone explains.

What an alert is worth

ISS crews report about one episode of upper-respiratory symptoms per person-year. At that rate, and with the detection above luck measured here, about 1 in 11 alert-tier alerts and about 1 in 37 notices would coincide with such an illness when daily steps are used, and about 1 in 25 and about 1 in 41 with the 7-day activity average the app uses. The other conditions Keel watches for would raise those shares, by an amount we cannot measure on real data. This is why a notice asks for a look, not for the medical officer.

False alerts over long quiet periods

People with no dated illness in their recording (146 people, 452 scored person-months; many were never confirmed healthy, so some alerts may be real). Notice tier: 0.99 false alerts per person-month (interval 0.88 to 1.10), with an alert open on 10% of quiet days. Alert tier: 0.24 per person-month, open on 2% of days; 54% of people never saw one. The noisiest fifth of people produced 63% of alert-tier false alerts, which is what pure chance would give (59%). Within this group, then, no individuals stand out as noisy. Between groups the rate does differ: non-Fitbit devices 0.74 per person-month (0.56 to 0.90) against 1.04 for Fitbit, and 1.17 for phase 1 people who reported no illness against 0.90 for phase 2 people not in the positives table.

Steps add much of the signal, and that is a caution. Heart rate alone is +22 points above luck; with steps, +34. Median daily steps fell from 7,554 to 4,210 in the five days from onset (89 episodes): people rest, or isolate after a positive test. A crew on a fixed exercise schedule may not show that, and because the data owners shifted every person's dates we cannot separate it from lockdowns.
At the alert tier the choice of activity feature matters. With daily steps Keel is +33 above luck; with the smoother 7-day average the app uses, +13, and its lead over a fixed threshold on the same sensors is +8 (-1 to 18). At the notice tier that lead is +24 (11 to 36).
Adjusting heart rate for the previous day's activity does not help (+35 against +34), so the app leaves it off.
Sleep is undecided. Too few people have sleep data.
Device matters. Fitbit: 72% flagged (+39 above luck, 89 episodes). Non-Fitbit devices: 39% (+10, 18 episodes, all Apple Watch); their overnight heart-rate samples are sparse in this release.
People who reported no illness in phase 1 saw 1.17 false alerts per person-month at the notice tier (interval 0.96 to 1.37).
It holds across cohorts. Trained on phase 1 and tested on phase 2: 62% flagged, +33 above luck, 0.87 false alerts per person-month. The reverse: 71%, +33, 1.11. Five different fold splits gave +34 to +36 above luck.
Retrospective and prospective phase 2 cases (the prospective ones were enrolled under the original study's own alerting): +38 and +34 above luck.
The window. -14..+7 days: 73% flagged, 42% by luck, gap +31 (22 to 40); -10..+4 days: 66% flagged, 33% by luck, gap +34 (24 to 43); -7..+0 days: 34% flagged, 18% by luck, gap +16 (6 to 25); -3..+0 days: 21% flagged, 9% by luck, gap +13 (5 to 21). The window was narrowed from −14..+7 to −10..+4 while the real-data evaluation was being written. The commit history cannot show that this came before the first run, and −10..+4 has the largest gap of the four, so treat the original window as the conservative figure.
Published comparison. The dataset's authors reported 63% of 32 cases detectable before symptoms with about 0.66 alarms per month, using hourly data and their own algorithm on phase 1. We did not reproduce early detection with daily features.

Limits you should know before trusting any of this
No open dataset has astronaut wearable time series. The real evaluation uses people on Earth; spaceflight physiology is represented only by published summary values and the bed-rest analog.
The real illness data has two indicators. Claims about HRV, oxygen, temperature, mood, reaction time, fluids and food rest on simulation with assumed effect sizes.
107 real episodes is a small sample; intervals are wide. Symptom dates are self-reported and shifted for privacy.
Thresholds for the full indicator set are calibrated on simulated missions and probably run over budget on real people.
Detection above luck is an upper bound on what physiology explains: pretend dates cannot be matched to the calendar, so travel, testing and isolation around a real onset are not reproduced. Heart rate alone (+22) is the safer figure.
The simulator's illness response was fitted on 38 of the 107 real episodes, so anything that passes through the simulator (the app's thresholds, the choice of detector) has seen those people; results on the 113 people it never saw are given beside the headline.
The four personal-baseline detectors perform alike at the notice tier. Keel runs the one picked on simulated training missions by a selection rule that was itself revised during review; the choice flipped once. The simple rule is +28 above luck at the notice tier and +26 at the alert tier.
