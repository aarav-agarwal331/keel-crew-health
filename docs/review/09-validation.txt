Does it work?

Every number on this page was produced by a script in the repository from data described here. Real data is used wherever it exists; everything else is simulated and labelled. Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals over people. Nothing was tuned on the people it was tested on.

What these results do not show. On real data Keel mostly flags illness on or just after the day symptoms start, not days before: only 41% of episodes had an alert open by the onset day, and a good part of that is luck. The real data is from people on Earth with a heart-rate watch, not astronauts. Detection of anything other than illness is tested in simulation only.

Indicators available: resting heart rate + daily steps. No HRV, oxygen or temperature exists in this data.

False alerts over long quiet periods

People with no reported illness (150 people, 439 person-months). At the notice tier Keel raised 0.99 false alerts per person-month (interval 0.89 to 1.09) and an alert was open on 10% of quiet days. At the alert tier: 0.23 per person-month, open on 2% of days, and 56% of people never saw one. False alerts are uneven: the noisiest fifth of people produced 65% of alert-tier false alerts.

Steps earn their place. Activity falls to about half after symptoms start, and adding it to heart rate raises detection.
Adjusting heart rate for the previous day's activity does not. It changed detection by -1 points, so the app leaves it off.
Sleep is undecided. Too few people have sleep data to tell.
Device matters. Fitbit: 75% detected. Apple Watch: 18% of 17 episodes; its overnight heart-rate samples are sparse in this release.
The window matters. -14..+7 days: 71% detected, 46% by luck; -10..+4 days: 65% detected, 37% by luck; -7..+0 days: 35% detected, 21% by luck; -3..+0 days: 22% detected, 11% by luck.
Published comparison. The dataset's authors reported 63% of 32 cases detectable before symptoms with about 0.66 alarms per month, using hourly data and their own algorithm on phase 1. Our daily-feature result is weaker on early warning and we did not reproduce theirs.

3. Is the simulator realistic?

Real values come from the validation half of each dataset; the calibration script only saw the other half. The simulator is tuned on one half of the real people and compared here with the other half.

Like-for-like check: simulated against real illness detection

On the two channels the real illness data has (resting heart rate and activity), with similar wear time, Keel detects 68% of simulated illnesses (interval 59 to 76%) against 68% of real symptom-dated episodes (interval 57 to 79%). At the alert tier: 37% simulated against 47% real. Where the two can be compared, the simulator is about as hard as reality, or slightly easier.

4. Gravity transitions on real NASA analog data

NASA Life Sciences Data Archive, public head-down-tilt bed-rest campaigns (UTMB campaigns 1 and 3, CFT70): daily nurse-taken pulse. 29 volunteers; the expected pulse shift was learned from 14 of them and tested on the other 15. Bed rest with the head tilted down is NASA's standard ground stand-in for weightlessness.

Transition	Volunteers	Flagged by a plain rolling baseline	Flagged by Keel's phase-aware baseline
Going into bed rest (head-down tilt)	15	2	1
Getting up after bed rest (return to upright)	14	9	4

A flag here means pulse at least 2.0 robust SD above the personal baseline on 2 of 3 consecutive days within 14 days after the transition. Getting up after weeks in bed raises the pulse, and a plain personal baseline calls that a problem in most volunteers. Telling the baseline that a transition happened removes about half of those flags. The numbers are small and this is pulse only, taken once a day by a nurse.

Limits you should know before trusting any of this
No open dataset has astronaut wearable time series. The real evaluation uses people on Earth; spaceflight physiology is represented only by published summary values and the bed-rest analog.
The real illness data has two indicators. Claims about HRV, oxygen, temperature, mood, reaction time, fluids and food rest on simulation with assumed effect sizes.
103 real episodes is a small sample; intervals are wide. Symptom dates are self-reported and shifted for privacy.
Thresholds for the full indicator set are calibrated on simulated missions, because no real data has the full set.
The detector that the app runs was chosen among four on simulated training missions; it also happened to be the best on real data, which we saw only afterwards.
