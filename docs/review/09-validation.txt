Does it work?

Every number here was produced by a script in the repository. Real data is used wherever it exists; everything else is simulated and labelled. Intervals are 95% bootstrap intervals over people (real data) or missions (simulation), with thresholds held fixed, which makes them somewhat narrower than they should be. Analysis choices such as the metric, the detection window and the detector selection rule were revised during development, so this is not a pre-registered analysis; every change is in the repository's decision log.

How much depends on how luck is measured (heart rate + daily steps, real thresholds)

Luck is estimated by placing pretend illness dates on quiet days and scoring them like real ones. Pretend dates handle missing data the same way as real ones (they carry the person's quiet-time gaps, not the extra unworn days illness brings) and are weighted to the device mix of the real episodes: luck 34%, gap +28. Matched to how far into the recording real onsets fall (a young baseline is noisier): 33%, gap +30. Using only people who had an episode: 33%, gap +30. Requiring a score on every day of a pretend window, as our earlier analysis did: 35%, gap +27. As a share of what luck leaves to be found, the gap is 43%.

What this cannot fix. Pretend events are placed on label-quiet days with the same missing data as real windows, and weighted to the device mix of the real episodes. They cannot be matched to the calendar (dates are shifted per person), so exposure, travel, testing and isolation near a real onset are not reproduced; detection above luck is therefore likely optimistic on what physiology alone explains.

What an alert is worth

ISS crews report about one episode of upper-respiratory symptoms per person-year. At that rate, and with the detection above luck measured here, about 1 in 13 alert-tier alerts and about 1 in 44 notices would be attributable to such an illness with daily steps and thresholds from real people; none (that tier never fires) and about 1 in 42 with the 7-day activity average; and about 1 in 22 and about 1 in 60 with thresholds from the simulator, which run over budget; on the watch maker's own resting heart rate, where the import model's false-alert rates are higher (assuming detection carries over unchanged, which was not measured), about 1 in 20 and about 1 in 70; and about 1 in 16 and about 1 in 67 for the heart-rate-only model imports run on. These are likely optimistic estimates of the share that is a respiratory illness (they assume one true alert per illness and false-alert rates measured on Earth): the sensitivity comes from mostly symptomatic COVID-19, in-flight colds are milder, and full-sensor thresholds running over budget (section 3) would lower them further. Alerts for the other conditions Keel watches for are not counted here; they cannot be measured on real data. For imported watch data the closest figures are the heart-rate-only ones on the watch maker's resting heart rate. For the on-board model no real-data figure exists; the shipped model file fed only import channels gives about 1 in 16 alert-tier alerts and about 1 in 29 notices, flattered because on those channels it under-alerts (0.12 and 0.35 false alerts a month against budgets of 0.25 and 1.00); at its budgets the same detection would give about 1 in 32 and 1 in 79. This is why a notice asks for a look, not for the medical officer.

How uncertain these are. Each share is a point estimate. Carrying the detection interval through the same formula, heart rate alone gives 1 in 11 to 1 in 29 alert-tier alerts, and 1 in 44 to 1 in 133 notices (the false-alert rate held fixed, so the real range is wider still).

By kind of illness (heart rate + daily steps, real thresholds; above luck, notice tier, then alert tier): COVID-19 dated by symptoms (79 episodes): +32 (20 to 42), +34 (23 to 45); COVID-19 dated only by a test (19 episodes): +19 (−4 to 42, includes zero), +2 (−10 to 18, includes zero); other illnesses, the closest stand-in for in-flight colds (9 episodes): +21 (−16 to 53, includes zero), +2 (−11 to 26, includes zero). In this configuration the alert-tier gain rests almost entirely on COVID-19 with dated symptoms, and for other illnesses no alert-tier benefit is shown; the heart-rate-only breakdown was not computed.

False alerts over long quiet periods (heart rate + daily steps, real thresholds)

People with no dated illness in their recording (146 people, 452 scored person-months; many were never confirmed healthy, so some alerts may be real). Notice tier: 0.95 false alerts per person-month (interval 0.85 to 1.08), with an alert open on 9% of quiet days. Alert tier: 0.23 per person-month, open on 2% of days; 61% of the 140 people with at least 30 quiet days never saw one. The noisiest fifth of people produced 74% of alert-tier false alerts, against 60% if everyone had the same rate: more concentrated than chance, so a few people carry much of the burden. So at the alert tier some individuals may be systematically noisier; at the notice tier the share matches a constant rate (44% against 44%). No formal test. Between groups (computed over all people, not only this group) the rates look different, though the intervals overlap: non-Fitbit devices 0.78 per person-month (0.60 to 0.95) against 1.02 for Fitbit, and 1.08 for phase 1 people who reported no illness against 0.89 for phase 2 people not in the positives table.

Not counted anywhere: alerts starting 11 to 21 days before an episode or 5 to 28 days after it fall outside the detection window but inside the exclusion zone. There were 74 at the notice tier (0.7 per episode) and 29 at the alert tier, so the false-alert rates understate the burden for people who were ill.

Limits you should know before trusting any of this
No open dataset has astronaut wearable time series. The real evaluation uses people on Earth; spaceflight physiology is represented only by published summary values and the bed-rest analog.
The real illness data has two indicators. Claims about HRV, oxygen, temperature, mood, reaction time, fluids and food rest on simulation with assumed effect sizes.
107 real episodes is a small sample; intervals are wide. Symptom dates are self-reported and shifted for privacy.
Thresholds for the full indicator set are calibrated on simulated missions and probably run over budget on real people.
Detection above luck is likely optimistic about what physiology explains: pretend dates cannot be matched to the calendar, so travel, testing and isolation around a real onset are not reproduced. Heart rate alone (+13 in the pre-set window, +19 in the narrower one) is the safer figure.
In simulation the on-board model barely depends on heart rate: removing resting heart rate leaves overall detection at 71% against 71% with all sensors; most of its simulated performance rests on sleep, exercise and check-in channels whose effects are assumed, while heart rate is the only channel tested on real data.
The simulator's heart-rate noise (spread, carry-over, person-to-person spread) was fitted on a separate half of quiet real people, who are also in the all-251 false-alert rates. Its illness response was fitted on 38 episode curves from 55 people; 47 of the 107 evaluated episodes belong to those people, so anything that passes through the simulator (the app's thresholds, the choice of detector) has seen those people; results on the 132 people not used by its fits are given beside the headline (not unseen: the 30% exercise cut and the 30% chance of an unworn night in simulated ground people were set with the real data in view).
The four personal-baseline detectors perform alike at the notice tier. Keel runs the one picked on simulated training missions by a selection rule that was itself revised during review; the choice has flipped twice, most recently to the simple rule after a sign error in the multivariate statistic was corrected. The simple rule is +28 above luck at the notice tier and +26 at the alert tier.
